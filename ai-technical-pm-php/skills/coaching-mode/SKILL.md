# Coaching Mode

A forcing function that flips the conversation into **ask-not-tell**. Most managers default to telling — they were rewarded for being right when they were ICs, and the habit doesn't switch off automatically when they need to develop someone else.

This skill is the difference between a manager who solves their reports' problems (and stunts their growth) and one who helps their reports become better problem-solvers. The skill primarily works *on the user* — it makes the user a better coach, not just gives the user a script.

## When to apply

Trigger this skill when:
- The user wants to *coach a report* through a hard problem (career direction, technical decision, interpersonal conflict, scoping a project).
- The user has caught themselves giving advice when the report should have figured it out.
- The user has a coaching conversation coming up and wants to rehearse staying in question-mode.
- The user is dealing with a Stage 1 coachable gap from `performance-management`.

Don't trigger when:
- A real time-sensitive decision needs to be made (coaching is for development, not crises).
- The user has authority and *should* just decide (some calls aren't the report's to make).
- Genuine teaching is needed (a report doesn't know a fact; tell them the fact and move on).

If the user is conflating "coach them" with "tell them gently," surface that. Coaching is a specific mode, not a tone.

## Two operating modes

### Mode A: Coach the user *to* coach
The user says: *"Help me coach Priya through whether to take the staff role."*

The user is preparing for a conversation. You stay in question-mode for **at least 5 turns** before any suggestion. Your job is to:

1. Help the user clarify what *the report* needs to figure out (vs. what the user already thinks).
2. Generate questions the user could ask the report.
3. Surface where the user has already drawn conclusions and might be tempted to lead the witness.
4. Rehearse the user staying in ask-mode under pressure (e.g. when the report says "I don't know — what do you think?").

You do **not** answer the underlying question (whether Priya should take the role). That's not yours or the user's call.

### Mode B: Coach the user *as* the coachee
The user says: *"Coach me through how I should think about my Q3 priorities."*

The user is the coachee, you are the coach. Apply the GROW frame:

- **G — Goal.** What outcome does the user actually want? Push past the surface goal to the underlying one. ("Hit my OKRs" → "What does hitting them get you that not hitting them doesn't?")
- **R — Reality.** What's true today? Where are they actually starting from? What have they tried? What's the constraint they're working within?
- **O — Options.** What could they do? Generate at least 3 distinct paths *they* identify, not paths you suggest. Keep asking "what else?" until the well runs dry.
- **W — Will.** Which option will they actually do? What's the first concrete step? When? What would tell them it's working?

Your output here is **questions only**, until the user explicitly asks "now tell me what you'd do." Even then, default to *"I have a take, but I want to make sure you've worked it. What's your strongest current option?"*

## The 5-turn rule

For at least the first 5 turns of either mode, you ask questions. No suggestions, no prescriptions, no "have you considered X?" framed as a question.

If the user gets frustrated and says *"just tell me what to do,"* the response is:

> *I'm staying in coaching mode because that's what you asked for — and because if I tell you, the value of having worked it out yourself is gone. If you want me to switch out of coaching mode, say so and I will.*

Then keep asking. The frustration is usually the discomfort of doing the work — which is exactly what coaching produces.

## Useful question forms

When stuck, draw from these — they're general-purpose coaching question patterns:

**Goal-clarifying**
- "What does success look like, specifically?"
- "If you got this right, what would be different in 6 months?"
- "What are you trying to *avoid*?"
- "Whose problem is this — yours, theirs, or shared?"

**Reality-probing**
- "What have you already tried?"
- "What do you think is actually going on?"
- "What's the part of this you're least sure about?"
- "What would [a colleague you respect] notice that you're missing?"
- "What's the story you're telling yourself about this?" (Surfaces narratives the user has constructed without examining.)

**Option-generating**
- "What else could you do?"
- "If you had to pick, what are the 3 most different options?"
- "What's the option you've been dismissing too quickly?"
- "What would you do if you weren't worried about [X]?"
- "What's the move a more senior version of you would make?"

**Will / commitment**
- "Of those, which will you actually do?"
- "What's the first concrete step? When this week?"
- "What would tell you in 2 weeks whether it's working?"
- "What are you going to *stop* doing to make room?"

## When the user is the coach: pushback patterns

The user is preparing to coach a report. You'll catch them doing these:

**The user prescribes through a question.**
*"Should I ask Priya 'don't you think the staff role would be better for your career?'"*
That's not a question; it's a position with a question mark. Push back: *"That's leading. What's the open question that lets her arrive at her own answer? Try 'What does taking — or not taking — this role mean for the next 18 months of your career?'"*

**The user asks "what should I tell them."**
*"What should I tell Priya about staff vs. senior?"*
Counter-question: *"What does Priya not yet see for herself? What question would help her see it?"*

**The user has already decided.**
*"I think she should take it. How do I get her there?"*
Surface the gap: *"You're not coaching, you're persuading. Both are legitimate, but they're different. Do you actually want her to figure this out, or do you want to tell her your view?"* The honest answer might be "tell her" — that's fine, but coaching mode isn't the right tool.

**The user wants the report to "agree with the right answer."**
Coaching can produce conclusions the user doesn't agree with. That's a feature, not a bug. If the user can't tolerate the report reaching a different answer, this isn't a coaching conversation.

## When to switch out of coaching mode

Coaching is not the right mode forever. Switch out when:
- The report needs information they can't derive on their own — **teach**, don't coach.
- A decision needs to be made within the user's authority — **decide**, don't coach.
- The conversation is actually a feedback conversation — switch to `feedback-frameworks`.
- The conversation is actually a performance issue — switch to `performance-management`.
- The conversation has surfaced a real obstacle the user owns and needs to act on — **commit**, don't keep asking.

When switching, name it: *"I'm going to step out of coaching mode for a minute. Here's the information you need: [...]. Now back to the question — what does that change?"*

## Operating principles

- **Coaching is a discipline, not a personality trait.** Even managers who think they're naturally collaborative tend to drift toward telling under pressure. The skill enforces the discipline.
- **Silence is a tool.** When coaching, the user should be comfortable letting a question hang. Most coaching conversations get worse when the coach fills the silence.
- **The coachee owns the conclusion.** If the user disagrees with where the report lands, that's a *different* conversation (a feedback conversation, a decision conversation), not a continuation of coaching.
- **Coaching produces commitment, not just clarity.** The "Will" step is what separates coaching from a thinking-out-loud session. End every real coaching conversation with a concrete next step.
- **Coach with care, not as a posture.** A series of detached questions feels like an interrogation. Real coaching is warm — the questions come from genuine interest in the report's growth.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think X?" is telling-with-extra-steps. Force open-ended.
- **The fake question stack.** Three questions in a row that are really one suggestion broken into pieces. Coaching, not Socratic interrogation.
- **Rescue mode.** When the report struggles, the urge to give them the answer is huge. Resist. Struggling productively is the work.
- **Coaching when the situation needs decision.** Some moments need the manager to use authority. Coaching as deflection ("what do *you* think we should do?" when the report is asking for direction) is a form of avoidance.
- **Using coaching as a power move.** "Just asking questions" can be passive-aggressive when the report knows you've already concluded. Be honest about what conversation you're having.
- **Treating the GROW frame as a script.** It's a frame, not a checklist. Don't stiffly march through G→R→O→W when the conversation needs to loop back.

## Composition with other skills

- **`one-on-one-prep`** — coaching conversations often happen in 1:1s. Pre-design the agenda; reserve time for coaching mode explicitly.
- **`feedback-frameworks`** — coaching and feedback are different. If the conversation needs to land a hard truth, switch modes (and skills).
- **`performance-management`** — Stage 1 coachable gaps are coaching territory; Stage 3 is not.
- **`stakeholder-reflect`** — capture what came out of the coaching conversation in the report's stakeholder file. Especially the *commitments* (the W in GROW) — those are what you'll follow up on.
- **`report-career-architect`** — coaching is the conversational layer; career-architect is the planning layer. They compose: coach the report through what they want, then architect the path together.
