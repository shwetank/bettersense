# Psychological Safety Builder

Build psychological safety on a team — the belief that one can speak up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes without punishment or humiliation. **Not just measure it, but build it.**

**Core premise (from Amy Edmondson via Google's Project Aristotle):** Psychological safety is the #1 factor in team effectiveness. Without it, teams don't surface problems, admit errors, or innovate. With it, teams learn faster, make better decisions, and execute reliably.

**What psychological safety is NOT:**
- Not "everyone is nice" — healthy conflict is safe
- Not "no accountability" — high standards + safety = high performance
- Not "no fear" — fear exists; safety means fear doesn't silence you
- Not "agreeable" — it's okay to disagree, challenge, say "I was wrong"

**What it IS:** Interpersonal risk-taking is rewarded, not punished. "If I speak up, something good or neutral will happen to me."

## When to apply

Trigger this skill when:
- Team members stay silent in meetings, then complain afterward
- No one admits mistakes; problems hidden until they explode
- People avoid asking "dumb questions"
- No one challenges the leader's opinion
- Retrospectives are superficial ("everything's fine")
- New members observe but don't participate
- After an incident — people reluctant to discuss root causes
- The user says: *"I need my team to tell me what's really going on"*

Skip when:
- The team already demonstrates psychological safety (evidenced by: people speak up, admit mistakes, challenge decisions respectfully)
- The user just wants a diagnostic (use `team-diagnosis` to assess safety levels)

## The four pillars of psychological safety

Based on Edmondson's work and practical implementation research:

1. **Leader vulnerability** — leaders admit mistakes, ask questions, show they don't have all the answers
2. **Response to bad news** — how does the team react when someone raises a concern or admits failure?
3. **Inclusion and belonging** — do all voices feel welcome, or only certain styles/personalities?
4. **Enabling conditions** — processes and rituals that reinforce safety day-to-day

The skill addresses each pillar with concrete actions.

## The team leader's actions (what to do this week)

### 1. Model vulnerability (Pillar 1)
**Why:** Safety starts at the top. If the leader is flawless, everyone else will pretend to be too.

**Actions:**
- In next team meeting, explicitly state: *"I was wrong about X. Here's what I missed."*
- Ask a question you don't know the answer to: *"What's the biggest risk in our current plan? I don't know and I need your perspectives."*
- Share a recent mistake and what you learned: *"I scheduled that meeting without an agenda — it was a waste of time. My bad. Here's how I'll fix it."*
- Say "I don't know" instead of guessing
- Solicit feedback on your leadership: *"What's one thing I should start, stop, or continue doing to help this team?"* (and actually listen)

**Frequency:** Do this 2–3 times per week until it's habitual.

### 2. Respond constructively to upward speech (Pillar 2)
**Why:** The single fastest way to kill safety is to punish the messenger. The single fastest way to build it is to reward speaking up.

**When someone raises a concern, bad news, or a mistake:**
- **Thank them immediately:** "Thanks for bringing this up. I'm glad you told me."
- **Listen without interrupting** — take notes, don't deflect
- **Separate the message from the delivery:** Even if tone is harsh, focus on content
- **Take action visibly:** "We'll look into this" → actually look into it and report back
- **Follow up privately:** "I appreciate you speaking up. Here's what I'm doing about it."

**Never do:**
- Shoot the messenger ("Why are you always negative?")
- Minimize ("That's not a big deal")
- Get defensive ("Well, if you had done your job...")
- Ignore (say nothing, change subject)
- Punish (give less desirable work, exclude from future discussions)

**Practice:** The skill gives a role-play script for the leader to rehearse their default reaction and replace it with the constructive one.

### 3. Actively solicit and honor all voices (Pillar 3)
**Why:** Safety isn't average; it's ensuring the quietest voice is heard.

**Actions:**
- **Round-robin in meetings:** "Let's hear from everyone before I summarize." Call on people by name, especially the quiet ones.
- **Pre-meeting input:** "Send me your thoughts before the meeting if you're not comfortable speaking." Then read them aloud in the meeting without attribution if needed.
- **Anonymous input option:** Use an anonymous form for sensitive topics; share aggregated results
- **Disrupt interruption pattern:** "Let Alice finish" or "Bob, you were saying something — finish your thought"
- **Value dissent:** "I want someone to argue the other side" — explicitly invite counterpoints
- **Credit publicly:** "Carol raised this concern last week and she was right — we should pivot"

**Measure:** Track participation distribution in meetings (who talks, how much). Aim for balanced airtime, not domination by 2–3 voices.

### 4. Create enabling structures (Pillar 4)
**Why:** Safety requires repeated positive experiences. Structures make that systematic.

**Rituals to introduce:**
- **Pre-mortems:** "Let's imagine this project failed — what could have gone wrong?" Everyone writes 1–2 risks anonymously, then discusses. Normalizes risk speech.
- **Blameless post-mortems:** When something breaks, focus on "what went wrong" not "who messed up." Use `the-postmortem-facilitator` agent if available.
- **Failure resume:** Encourage team members to keep a "what I learned from failures" list; share in 1:1s and team meetings
- **Appreciation round:** Start or end meetings with "one thing I appreciated about a teammate this week" — builds trust, counters negativity bias
- **Ask me anything (AMA) sessions:** Leader or guest answers any question (with opt-out for truly personal)
- **Decision debriefs:** "Let's review decisions we made 2 weeks ago — what did we learn?" Normalizes being wrong

**Process changes:**
- **No-interruption meeting policy:** Use hand-raising in virtual meetings; physical hand-raising in person
- **Decisions documented with rationale:** "We decided X because Y" — allows future critique without finger-pointing
- **Assumption testing:** "What are we assuming that we haven't validated?" scheduled quarterly
- **Safe escalation path:** Anonymous channel to leader's manager if leader is the safety problem

## Assessing current safety level

The skill provides a quick diagnostic (not a substitute for `team-diagnosis` but faster):

**Anonymous 5-question pulse (1–5 scale):**
1. If I make a mistake on this team, it is used against me
2. It is safe to speak up and disagree with my teammates
3. Problems and concerns can be raised without fear
4. My unique skills and perspectives are valued
5. This team is inclusive; I feel I belong

Average score ≤ 3 indicates significant safety work needed.

**Behavioral signals the skill looks for:**
- Silence in meetings, especially after a controversial point
- "Meetings after the meeting" (real talk happens offline)
- No one admits errors in retros
- Leader's opinion announced first, then everyone agrees
- "That won't work" said as joke, not serious challenge

## Addressing common safety blockers

### "Our leader is the problem"
If the leader's behavior (dominating, punishing, defensive) undermines safety:
- Leader runs this skill on themselves first; commits to specific behavior changes
- Leader solicits 360 feedback (anonymous) on safety behaviors
- Leader models vulnerability publicly and reports on progress in team meetings
- Escalate (via `stakeholder-reflect` on the leader) if no change

### "We're too busy for safety rituals"
Safety isn't overhead — it's risk reduction. Unspoken problems cost more time later.
- Start with 5-minute pre-mortem at start of major project (cheap, high ROI)
- Add 2-minute "round-robin concerns" to existing meeting agenda
- Document one safety win per month: *"We caught X early because someone spoke up"*

### "We already trust each other"
Most teams overestimate their safety. Test with:
- *"When was the last time someone openly disagreed with the leader?"*
- *"What's a mistake someone recently admitted?"*
- If answers are "never" or "can't remember," safety is lower than perceived.

### "Conflict is toxic here"
Distinguish **task conflict** (ideas) from **relationship conflict** (personal). Encourage task conflict, suppress relationship conflict.
- Norm: "Attack ideas, not people"
- Facilitator intervenes when辩论 gets personal: "Let's refocus on the problem, not the person"
- Debrief after heated discussion: "That was a good debate. What did we learn?"

## Integrating with the 3X3 team framework

- **Team Charter:** Include a "psychological safety norm" explicitly: e.g., *"We assume good intent. We speak up early. Mistakes are learning opportunities."*
- **Team Check-in:** Add a safety pulse question: *"On a scale of 1–5, how safe did you feel speaking up this sprint?"*
- **Team Close-gap:** If safety is low, that's the gap to close. Small step: *"We will each share one mistake in retro without judgment"*

## Leader self-check (run weekly)

After each team interaction:
1. **Did I model vulnerability this week?** (admitted uncertainty or error)
2. **Did I reward or punish speaking up?** (Was the last person who challenged me thanked or shot down?)
3. **Did all voices get airtime?** (Who talked 70%? Who stayed silent?)
4. **Did we surface any bad news early?** Or did problems fester?

If two or more "no," run a repair conversation at next 1:1 or team check-in: *"I noticed X. I want to improve. Here's what I'll do differently."*

## Operating principles

- **Safety ≠ comfort.** You can be safe and challenged. You can be unsafe and comfortable (silent).
- **It's the leader's job to build it.** Safety doesn't happen accidentally; it's designed.
- **Start small.** One vulnerable admission, one thanked concern, one pre-mortem — compound over time
- **Safety enables accountability.** High standards + safety = people hold each other accountable without fear
- **Repair quickly.** Safety breaches damage trust; acknowledge and repair within 24h
- **Safety is not everything else being perfect.** You can have safety amid stress, ambiguity, and failure — that's when you need it most

## Anti-patterns to flag

- **Safety theater:** "We're all friends here" while dissent is punished
- **Safety as permissive:** No standards, no accountability — that's complacency, not safety
- **One-off events:** One offsite doesn't build lasting safety; requires consistent behavior
- **Leader as victim:** "I can't be vulnerable, they'll lose respect" — false; vulnerability builds respect
- **Ignoring exclusion:** One person consistently interrupted or ignored — safety problem
- **Safety monitoring as surveillance:** Don't track who speaks up *to catch silent people* — track to ensure all voices are *invited and valued*
